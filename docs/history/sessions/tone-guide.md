# STEAL THE MOON — Tone Guide & Sample Passages

*For the prose writer. Read this once, internalize the voice, then go write the actual story. This is a style sheet, not a script.*

---

## 1. THE VOICE

**One sentence:** The heist of the century, told by an unhinged true-crime documentary narrator who is also a space nerd who genuinely did the math — and is now delivering it as a *past true event*, like a declassified history the archives finally coughed up.

**The register:** archival/declassified-documentary with "recovered footage" energy. Everything already happened. We are historians of an absurd crime. That distance is what makes it funny instead of scary: the catastrophe is over (or hasn't finished yet — see temporal layers), the paperwork survived, and the paperwork is *hilarious*.

**Three temporal layers — rotate them deliberately:**

1. **PAST EVENT (the crime):** tense, propulsive, heist-movie. "At 04:12 UTC the torch lit and the most audacious burglary in history began."
2. **PRESENT EXPLANATION (the doc):** deadpan lecturer mode. This is where the real physics lives — DART numbers, Roche limits, delta-v budgets — delivered like a tour guide who cannot believe any of this is real.
3. **FUTURE CONSEQUENCE (the dread, played for laughs):** the stuff that hasn't blown up *yet*. "As of this recording, it has not fragmented. The committee wishes to stress the word *yet*."

**Core promise:** goofy but never stupid. The science is REAL and treated with genuine awe. The joke is never "space is dumb" — the joke is "a bunch of primates did *this*, on purpose, with spreadsheets." Punch up at institutions, bureaucracies, and physics. Never down at people. No real persons depicted, ever.

**Meme-awareness without meme-rot:** the narrator knows what "yeet" means and will use it exactly once per concept, then retire it. References should feel like a smart friend texting you, not a brand account. Aim for *timeless-funny*, not *2026-funny*.

**Unscary rule:** the Kessler-cascade stakes stay real — that's the thriller engine — but the *telling* stays playful. Think: the bomb under the table is discussed with the energy of a cooking show host explaining that the soufflé might explode. It might. It really might. Anyway, next step: mining the soufflé.

## 2. RHYTHM & CADENCE

Read everything aloud. If you can't say it in one breath with a grin, cut it. Techniques:

**1. Staccato, then roll.** Short punches to land the fact, then one long rolling sentence that piles on detail until it topples.
> *They stole a moon. Not a rock. Not a satellite. A whole moon — all 396 kilometers of it, Herschel crater and all, towed across the solar system like a shopping cart with a rocket strapped to it.*

**2. Rule of three (third one twists).** Two straight items, third one breaks the pattern.
> *The cover story had everything: a technical term ("expected settling"), a confident spokesperson (Captain Ren), and absolutely no relationship to reality.*

**3. Repetition with a twist.** Repeat a phrase, then mutate it on the callback.
> *The moon burns pieces of itself to travel. It burns its own ice to cross the dark. It is, and the archives confirm this phrasing, eating itself to get here faster.*

**4. Bureaucratic bathos.** Build to something huge, then puncture with official language.
> *It was the greatest theft in human history, and the press release called it "an asset relocation."*

**5. The list that escalates past reason.** Start sensible, end absurd. The escalation IS the joke.
> *ARGUS needed a Jupiter assist. Then a Saturn assist. Then assists off Uranus, Neptune, three of their moons, and — per the recovered logs — "one (1) extremely rude maneuver around Triton that we do not discuss."*

**6. Em-dash ambushes.** Interrupt yourself mid-thought with the funnier, truer thought.
> *The braking burn was nominal — nominal in the sense that a 40-kilometer crack propagating through the ice shell is technically a thing that can happen.*

**7. Deadpan caption vs. unhinged narrator.** Let the "archival" voice and the narrator voice argue. The friction is comedy.
> *ARCHIVE CAPTION: "Diagram 7-B: Parking orbit (stable)." NARRATOR: It was stable in the same way a piano is stable while falling off a roof.*

## 3. SAMPLE LINES & MICRO-PASSAGES

**The theft reveal:**
> Somebody stole a moon. Not "moved." Not "nudged." Stole — towed it a billion kilometers sunward, parked it next to Earth, and started strip-mining it for helium-3 before anyone finished their coffee.

**The Death Star comparison:**
> Mimas is 396 kilometers of grey cratered ice with a 130-kilometer impact crater punched in its face, which means it has looked like the Death Star since approximately forever, and nobody — *nobody* — thought to guard it. You don't lock up the Death Star. The Death Star locks up *you*. They counted on this.

**The ten-trillion-DARTs line:**
> NASA's DART mission proved you can shove an asteroid with a spacecraft. Great. To move Mimas one meter per second that way, you'd need ten trillion DARTs — which is less a mission architecture and more a confession that you need a bigger idea.

**The Long Torch:**
> The bigger idea was the Long Torch: a fusion drive that burns, per the declassified estimates, roughly two million years of humanity's total energy output. It is the most expensive lighter ever built, and they used it to steal a moon.

**The Roche limit:**
> Park a moon too close to Earth and tides rip it apart — the Roche limit, about 26,000 kilometers up, the altitude at which physics files for divorce. Cross it and your moon becomes a ring. A very pretty, extremely career-ending ring.

**The parking orbit:**
> So they parked it far outside the kill zone: a distant retrograde orbit, 70,000 kilometers out, where Mimas hangs in the sky two-thirds the width of the Moon. Earth got a second moon. Nobody voted on it. It just showed up, like a roommate's couch.

**ARGUS's simulations:**
> No human plotted the trajectory. A human *can't* — it threads gravity assists off all four gas giants and a selection of their moons, and changing one burn by one second rewrites the entire cascade. ARGUS ran trillions of simulations to find the one thread through the maze. The station AGI did the math. The humans just signed the paperwork and tried to look calm.

**The Herschel Slip:**
> During the final braking burn, a forty-kilometer fracture tore outward from Herschel's rim and dove deep into the ice shell. The official statement called it "expected settling." The ice shell, for the record, had not been consulted, and did not agree.

**The cover-up, Ren-style:**
> Captain Ren is a professional. She tells the official story the way a vault door closes: completely, finally, and with the clear implication that asking again would be embarrassing for *you*.

**Qiao and the 72 hours:**
> Co-pilot Qiao's flight record contains a 72-hour gap labeled only "Saturn extraction operations." Somewhere in those three days, the first crack started. He has never said when. His smile, in every recovered photo from that week, is doing a lot of heavy lifting.

**The hearing (Sera Voss):**
> Defector Sera Voss sat before the committee and contradicted the official story point by point, with exhibits, with timestamps, with the serene energy of a woman who had been waiting years to say "per my last email" to a government.

**Earth-side panic (news ticker):**
> TICKER: "OFFICIALS URGE CALM." TICKER: "SECOND MOON VISIBLE TONIGHT, 'WEIRDLY BIG.'" TICKER: "EXPERTS SAY RING OF DEBRIS 'UNLIKELY,' ADD 'PLEASE STOP ASKING.'" TICKER: "STOCKS — YOU KNOW WHAT, NEVER MIND."

**The moon burns pieces of itself:**
> Trimming the trajectory took mass drivers — cannons that threw Mimas's own ice overboard as reaction mass. Read that again. The moon burned pieces of itself to travel. It ate its own body to cross the solar system, and it *still* arrived hungry.

**The fast-arrival insight:**
> Here's the trick the other side missed: Mimas never needed to arrive slow. A fast arrival just means a bigger capture orbit — you don't brake harder, you brake *wider*. All it ever needed was to get swung out of Saturn and start falling inward. Gravity does the commuting. You just have to catch a falling moon, which is exactly as easy as it sounds.

**The shepherd fleet:**
> Riding shotgun: a shepherd fleet of captured asteroids, flying formation as gravity tractors — tugging the moon with nothing but their own weight — plus DART-style impactors for trim. It was a heist crew. Every heist needs a crew. This one just happened to be made of rocks.

**Footnote-style aside (in-flow example):**
> The interior is a small rocky core, about 200 kilometers across, wrapped in a thick shell of ice — possibly hiding a young subsurface ocean, per 2024 research, which means we may have stolen a moon with *feelings*.*[1]*
>
> *[1] Not a scientific term. The committee apologizes.*

## 4. PLAYFUL FOOTNOTES

The archivist's voice: fussy, pedantic, barely holding it together. Use footnotes as the straight man the narrator bounces off.

*[1] The committee would like it noted that "yeeting" is now an official orbital-mechanics term. The vote was 7–2. The dissenters have been assigned to debris-tracking duty.*

*[2] "Expected settling" appears 214 times in the official record. The ice shell was never interviewed.*

*[3] Ten trillion is the conservative estimate. The optimistic estimate is worse.*

*[4] ARGUS declined to comment, citing "ongoing operations." ARGUS is a computer. It does not get to do this. And yet.*

*[5] Captain Ren's statement lasted four minutes and contained zero verbs in the past tense. Linguists are still arguing about what this means.*

*[6] Qiao's 72 hours remain classified. The archivist has filed eleven requests. The archivist has received eleven identical photographs of a filing cabinet.*

*[7] The Roche limit is named for Édouard Roche, who calculated exactly how close you can get before everything falls apart, and who presumably never imagined his limit would be tested by grand theft moon.*

*[8] "Asset relocation" is doing a tremendous amount of work in that sentence. The archivist has flagged it for review. The review is scheduled for never.*

*[9] As of this recording, Mimas has not fragmented. The committee wishes to stress the word "recording."*

*[10] Helium-3 is extremely valuable, extremely rare, and — per the mining consortium's prospectus — "basically worth a moon." Their words. We're just the archive.*

## 5. WHAT TO AVOID

- **Don't make light of real tragedies.** The Kessler-cascade stakes are fictional-future; real disasters, real deaths, real suffering are never punchlines. The goof lives in the absurdity of the *situation*, never in anyone's pain.
- **Don't punch at real nations' people.** The rival power is an institution — a program, a committee, a press office. Mock bureaucracies, acronyms, and official euphemisms all you want. Never mock ordinary people, and never traffic in national stereotypes. Qiao is a person, not a punchline; his conflict is the tragedy inside the comedy.
- **No real persons depicted.** Historical figures stay in footnotes (Roche gets a pass — he's been dead since 1883 and it's about math). Nobody living, nobody recognizable, nobody whose lawyer could find us.
- **Don't let the goof eat the science.** The physics is the straight man; if the facts get sloppy, the jokes stop landing. Every number in this guide is load-bearing. If you invent a number, footnote that you invented it.
- **Don't do dread without a joke nearby.** The thriller bones need the scary thing to be real — but this is the *goofy* release. Rule of thumb: any passage about fragmentation, cascade, or the Slip gets at least one pressure-release valve within three sentences. Scary, then silly, then scary again. That's the rhythm of the whole piece.
- **Don't explain the joke in narration.** The narrator never says "get it?" or winks at the audience. The footnotes wink. The narrator is deadly serious about absurd things, which is the entire engine.
- **Don't overuse any single bit.** "Yeet" once per concept. The Death Star comparison lands twice max — then it becomes furniture, which is fine, furniture is allowed, but stop pointing at it. Callbacks are seasoning, not the meal.
- **Don't both-sides the cover-up.** The official story is false and the story knows it. Ren is sympathetic — she's protecting her crew, her program, maybe her life — but the narrative never pretends "expected settling" might be true. Dramatic irony is the fuel: the reader knows, Ren knows the reader knows, and still the vault door closes.
- **Don't rush the heist.** The theft is the fun part. Linger on the logistics — the shepherd fleet, the mass drivers, the trillion simulations. Competence is funny. Watching extremely competent people do an extremely absurd thing is the whole show.
